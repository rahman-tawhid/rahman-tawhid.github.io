You Are Not Behind

The mid-market CEO’s path to effective AI. Nearly half of all leaders feel underprepared — and that feeling is almost entirely wrong. Three questions, four stages, and the architecture that turns a first experiment into a compounding capability.

Tawhid Rahman2026~20 min read

Let me describe a scene that has played out, with minor variations, in approximately nine thousand mid-market boardrooms over the past eighteen months.

You are sitting at your desk. It is either very early in the morning or somewhat too late in the evening — the hours when executives do their actual thinking. You have just read your third LinkedIn post this week about how artificial intelligence is transforming everything, authored by someone whose company is either six months old or six hundred billion dollars in market capitalisation, and either way bears no resemblance to yours. Your board has asked about your AI strategy. Your competitors appear to be doing things with AI that make your operations look like they are being run with carrier pigeons and a particularly well-organised filing cabinet.

You feel, in the technical parlance of modern executive psychology, behind.

I would like to suggest, with the confidence that comes from having helped mid-market companies navigate exactly this moment, that this feeling is almost entirely wrong. Not because the pressure is imaginary — it is not — but because the gap between where you are and where you need to be is significantly smaller than the gap between where you are and where LinkedIn thinks you should be.

The Middle Market Problem

There is an enormous amount of writing about AI in business. Almost none of it is about you.

The enterprise literature assumes you have a Chief Digital Officer, a data science team, and a technology budget that arrives in a briefcase. The small business literature assumes you can solve your problems with ChatGPT and a credit card. You — the CEO of a company with fifty to five hundred employees, doing real revenue in a real market — fall into the gap between these two bodies of advice like a person who is too tall for the children’s rides and too short for the adults’.

RSM’s 2025 Middle Market AI Survey found that ninety-one percent of mid-market executives report using AI in some form. But seventy percent need outside help to realise value from it, and ninety-two percent have experienced meaningful implementation challenges. This is the mid-market condition. You are too substantial to ignore AI and too constrained to approach it the way the articles suggest.

What the Data Actually Says About Your Position

LHH’s 2026 View from the C-Suite report surveyed over twenty-five hundred companies worldwide: AI and emerging technology is now the number one perceived leadership development gap globally, rising seven places in a single year. Nearly half of all leaders cite it as a top priority.

Read that again. Nearly half of the world’s executives feel underprepared for AI. You are not behind. You are exactly where almost everyone is.

PwC’s 2026 CEO Survey found that fifty-six percent of companies have captured neither meaningful revenue growth nor cost savings from their AI investments. The implication is clear: throwing money at AI is not a strategy. It is a hope dressed in a business case.

The companies succeeding with AI are not succeeding because they started earlier or spent more. They are succeeding because they asked a different question. Not “How do we implement AI?” but “Which of our business processes generate the most friction, and can a machine reduce that friction meaningfully?”

The Three Questions That Actually Matter

In my experience, the mid-market CEO needs to answer three questions about AI. Only three. Everything else is implementation detail.

Question One: Where are your people doing work that a machine could do better?

This is not about replacing people. It is about identifying the hours that your best employees spend on tasks that are beneath their abilities. The operations manager who spends Mondays manually reconciling data from three systems. The sales team that researches prospects by opening seventeen browser tabs. The finance team that produces the monthly report by copying numbers from one spreadsheet to another with the enthusiasm of a person doing penance.

These are taxes on your human capital — costs that exist not because they must, but because nobody has yet found a way to eliminate them. AI eliminates them with a reliability and a cost structure that make the decision feel less like innovation and more like common sense.

Question Two: What decisions are you making with insufficient information?

Mid-market companies make decisions constantly. Pricing. Hiring. Inventory. Market entry. Almost all of them are made with less information than the decision deserves, because gathering more information takes time that does not exist.

AI does not make these decisions for you. What it does is compress the time between asking a question and having a useful answer. What are our customers actually complaining about? Which products are underperforming? Where is our cash flow most vulnerable? These questions all have answers sitting in your data, invisible to humans because humans cannot hold ten thousand data points in their heads simultaneously.

Question Three: What would your company look like with fifty percent more capacity?

This is the question that separates companies that use AI from companies that are transformed by it. Not “Can we do the same things faster?” but “What would we do with the time we get back?”

The answer is almost always the same: pursue the opportunities you already know exist but cannot reach because your people are fully allocated. The partnership that would require dedicated relationship management. The market that would require sustained research. The product improvement that would require someone to actually analyse the feedback. AI creates the capacity to pursue them.

Every AI implementation decision maps to one of three questions. Answer them in order.

The Mistake That Costs More Than Any Technology

There is one mistake I see mid-market CEOs make more than any other, and it is not choosing the wrong AI tool. It is treating AI as a technology project.

Technology projects have budgets, timelines, and completion dates. They are delegated to IT. AI is not a project. It is a capability. The difference is the difference between buying a gym membership and becoming fit. The membership is a one-time decision. The fitness requires ongoing engagement.

The companies in McKinsey’s top tier did not buy better AI. They built organisations that know how to learn from AI, iterate on AI, and integrate AI into how they think about operations. This is an organisational skill, not a technical one.

The practical implication: your first AI implementation will be imperfect. It should be. Its purpose is not to deliver flawless results. Its purpose is to teach your organisation how to work with AI, so that the second implementation is better, and the third is better still.

What Starting Actually Looks Like

It does not look like a strategy deck. It does not look like a board presentation. It looks like this: pick one process that frustrates your team. One. Not three. Automate it. Measure what changes. Learn what works and what does not. Then pick another process. Repeat.

This approach lacks the drama of a transformation programme. It is also, according to every data set available, how companies actually succeed with AI.

From Process to Pipeline: The Technical Anatomy

The advice to “pick one process and automate it” is correct but insufficient in roughly the same way that “cook something” is correct but insufficient advice for someone standing in a kitchen for the first time. The what is clear. The how requires architecture.

Four stages: decompose the process, build composable skills, assemble into an automated pipeline, instrument with an observation layer.

Stage 1: Process decomposition

Take the process you selected and break it into constituent tasks at the level of granularity where you can point to each one and say: “A machine could do this” or “This requires a human.”

A sales research process decomposes into: identify the target company, gather firmographic data, analyse their technology stack and pain points, assess fit against your criteria, score the prospect, draft a personalised outreach message. Six tasks. A human adds genuine value in perhaps two — assessing strategic fit and approving outreach. The remaining four are research, pattern matching, and text generation: tasks where a machine is not merely adequate but superior.

The decomposition reveals the human-judgment boundary — the line where automation should stop and a decision-maker should be presented with a summary and a choice. Getting this boundary right is more important than getting the automation right.

Stage 2: Skill construction

Each automatable task becomes a skill — a self-contained module with a defined input, a defined output, and a quality standard. The company research task becomes a research skill. The prospect scoring task becomes a scoring skill. The outreach drafting task becomes a writing skill with brand voice constraints.

The skill carries metadata: what it expects, what it produces, what quality bar its outputs must clear, and a version history. This metadata is not bureaucratic overhead. It is the interface contract that allows skills to compose — to connect in pipelines where the output of one becomes the input of the next, automatically and reliably.

Stage 3: Pipeline assembly

Skills connect into pipelines — automated workflows that execute on schedules or in response to triggers. A typical first pipeline: a scheduled trigger fires at 7 AM, a research skill gathers intelligence on target companies, a scoring skill evaluates each one, the top-scoring prospects are enriched with additional research, outreach drafts are generated, and results are delivered to the sales board with a mobile notification for high-value targets that require approval.

The pipeline replaces three hours of morning manual work. The freed hours are not incremental efficiency. They are capacity — capacity the team can redirect toward relationship-building and strategic work that no pipeline can automate.

Stage 4: The observation layer

Once a pipeline is running, instrument it: build the system that watches it and tells you how it performs, what it is learning, and where it needs adjustment.

The observation layer is distinct from the pipeline. The pipeline executes. The observation layer synthesises. It does not modify the pipeline — it reads from it. This separation prevents the most dangerous failure mode: a system that modifies its own inputs based on its own outputs, creating invisible feedback loops.

Over time, the observation layer transforms your first modest automation into a genuine operational capability. The data reveals patterns that were invisible when the process was manual. These insights feed back into skill refinement, which feeds back into pipeline performance, which feeds back into observation. The loop compounds.

The company that started with one automated process finds, six months later, that it has built something its competitors cannot replicate by simply purchasing the same tools — because the value is not in the tools. It is in the accumulated learning encoded in the architecture.

The Path Forward

The mid-market CEO who does this — who starts with one process, decomposes it, builds the skills, assembles the pipeline, and instruments the observation layer — will find, within twelve months, that the feeling of being behind has been replaced by something far more useful: the feeling of knowing exactly what to do next.

Which, for a CEO, is the only competitive advantage that never depreciates.
