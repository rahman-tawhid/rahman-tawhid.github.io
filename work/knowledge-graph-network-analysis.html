Network Analysis of a Personal Knowledge Graph

Every note in an Obsidian vault, parsed into a typed graph, projected onto the notes that carry ideas, partitioned three ways and tested against a null model. What the structure says about how one reader files what he reads, and what it says about the filing system itself.

Tawhid Rahman is the CEO of Softograph, a software company he founded in 2012. The vault analysed here is his own reading and thinking notebook, kept since 2020. The data is real, the code is in the last section, and seven notes with personal reflections are masked in every labelled output.

The data

The vault is an Obsidian folder of markdown files. Notes arrive in 00 Inbox, and the ones I rework and keep are promoted to 20 Cards. 10 Atlas holds index notes: one per topic, one per author, one per book. Every concept note starts from a template whose frontmatter asks for a type, a status, a list of topics, a source and an author, each written as a wikilink.

That template decides the shape of the graph more than anything I wrote. It creates two layers. The concept layer is 464 notes that carry ideas: 317 in the inbox, 97 cards and 50 quotes. The index layer is 962 notes that exist so the concept notes have something to point at, and almost all of them are empty. Of 487 book notes, 486 have fewer than 20 words. All 268 topic notes and all 207 author notes are empty files. They are nodes because something links to them, not because anything is in them.

The pipeline below turns that folder into something that can be measured. Each step is a script, and each number in this article comes out of one of them.

Seven modelling decisions

A knowledge graph does not arrive as a graph. It arrives as text with brackets in it, and every step from text to network is a choice that changes the result. I made seven of them. Each one is below, with the alternative I rejected and the measurement that decided it. Where the alternative was cheap to run, I ran it, and the sensitivity table later in the piece shows how far each one moves the answer.

Figure 3. Methodology decision tree. Solid: the option taken. Dashed: the alternative, with the evidence against it.

Edges from frontmatter as well as body text. Only 356 of the kept edges come from links I typed into a paragraph. The rest come from the topics:, author: and source: fields. A body-only graph leaves 257 of 464 concept notes with no edge at all, so it describes the few notes I cross-referenced by hand and nothing else.

Metadata links dropped. Every note links to its type and its status. Type/Book receives 487 links and Type/Note 403. Kept, those two notes become the largest hubs in the vault and every note sits two steps from every other, which is true and says nothing.

Unresolved links reported, not modelled. Obsidian draws a node for a link to a note that does not exist. There are 32 such links to 29 targets, seven of them pasted images and most of the rest one-off tags. Modelling them would add 29 degree-one nodes and no information.

Partition the concept projection, not the full graph. The full graph is heterogeneous: concepts point at topics, books point at authors. Community detection on it mostly rediscovers the book catalogue. So the concept notes become the nodes, and two notes are tied when they share a topic, a source book or an author, or when one links to the other.

Weight shared entities by resource allocation. Two notes that share philosophy have shared something 68 concept notes also share. Two notes that share Kerckhoffs's principle have shared something rare. Each shared entity with k concept notes contributes 1/(k−1) to the tie, the resource-allocation index of Zhou, Lü and Zhang (2009). Direct note-to-note links add 1. Without the discount, the two big topics swamp everything and the partition collapses from 14 clusters to 6.

Leiden, checked against Louvain and Infomap. Leiden guarantees connected communities, which Louvain does not. I ran it with 20 seeds and kept the best. The seeds agree with each other at NMI 0.92, Louvain agrees at 0.88, and Infomap, which optimises a different objective, agrees at 0.78 while splitting the vault into 43 finer modules.

A null model that respects the bipartite structure. Projections manufacture clustering: every topic becomes a clique. An Erdős-Rényi comparison would make any projection look spectacular. The fair null shuffles which concept notes hold which topics, books and authors while preserving how many each note holds and how many notes each entity has, then re-projects and re-partitions.

What the raw graph shows

The inbox is a capture queue, and promotion tracks elaboration

Half of the inbox is stubs. The median inbox note has 13 words of body text; the median card has 255. That gap is the clearest signal in the vault, and it matches how the system is meant to work: capture fast, elaborate later, promote what survives.

Figure 4. Maturity by note class.

The template also asks for a My Thoughts section. It gets filled in 20% of inbox notes and 18% of cards. A Fisher exact test gives p = 0.66. Personal commentary is not what gets a note promoted. Length is. The status field says less still: 427 of 464 concept notes are marked WIP, including 79 of the 97 cards. It is set by the template and never changed.

The Bengali library is five islands

The graph has 120 connected components. The giant one holds 1,057 notes. The next five are all the same shape: one Bengali author and every title of theirs in the library, with no topics attached and so no path to anything else. Humayun Ahmed with 62 titles, Syed Mujtaba Ali with 50, Satyajit Ray with 46, Muhammed Zafar Iqbal with 27, Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay with 12. Together that is 197 books, 40% of the library, joined to the rest of the vault by nothing.

Figure 5. Largest components. The islands are catalogue imports that were never tagged.

Books and ideas use different vocabularies

Book entries and concept notes both point at topic notes, so the topic layer ought to be where reading and thinking meet. It mostly is not. Books use 93 topics, concept notes use 199, and they share 59. Fiction has 65 book links and no concept links. Machine-learning has 30 concept links and no book links. The book topics came from a catalogue; the concept topics came from me.

Figure 6. Topic vocabularies of the two layers. Jaccard 0.25.

Heavy tails, not a power law

Backlink counts are wildly unequal. Philosophy receives 97, psychology 89, fiction 65, and the median linked-to note receives one. It is tempting to call that scale-free. The test says otherwise. Fitting with the method of Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (2009) gives a power-law exponent of 1.88, but the likelihood-ratio comparisons favour a lognormal (R = -2.50, p = 0.013) and a truncated power law (R = -3.17, p = 7e-05). The pure power law does beat an exponential by a wide margin. That is the common result for real networks, as Broido and Clauset (2019) showed at scale: heavy-tailed, rarely scale-free.

Figure 7. Complementary cumulative in-degree distribution, log-log, with maximum-likelihood fits.

How the vocabulary grows

Creation dates come from the template's created: field where it exists (290 notes) and from file creation time otherwise (174). Two spikes, December 2025 and June 2026, include batch imports, which is why the monthly chart is shown but not modelled.

The more reliable measure is how fast the topic vocabulary grows as notes accumulate. Heaps' law predicts V(n) = K·nβ with β below 1 when new notes increasingly reuse existing topics. The fit gives β = 0.68. New topics keep arriving, at a slowing rate. The vocabulary has not saturated, which is consistent with a reader still widening rather than deepening.

Figure 8. Monthly creation and Heaps' law for topic vocabulary.

The concept projection

Projected, the 464 concept notes form 9,587 weighted ties, 262 of them from direct links. 63 notes have no tie at all, almost all of them empty inbox captures, and the giant component holds 399. Density is 0.089.

The unweighted giant component has average clustering 0.73 and average shortest path 2.34, against 0.12 and 1.88 for a random graph of the same size. That gives a small-world coefficient σ = 4.9, and it is mostly an artefact. The fair bipartite null has clustering 0.59 on its own, because projection turns every shared topic into a triangle factory. The honest statement is that the projection is modestly more clustered than its own null, not that my notes form a small world. The diameter is 5.

Two decisions carry the result. Dropping the resource-allocation weights halves the number of clusters and leaves a partition that shares less than half its information with the baseline. Dropping the handful of body links I typed by hand costs almost a third of the structure, which says those few deliberate links do more work per edge than the template fields. Merging duplicate topic labels, removing the two biggest hubs, or using topics alone barely moves it.

Roles and bridges

A cluster says where a note belongs. A role says how it belongs. Guimerà and Amaral (2005) classify every node by two numbers: z, how much more connected it is inside its own cluster than its clustermates, and P, how evenly its ties spread across clusters. The thresholds form a decision tree.

Figure 10. Role assignment as a decision tree, with the count of notes that land in each leaf.

Figure 11. Every note in the giant component on the role plane.

Most notes, 257 of 399, are peripheral: mainly tied into their own cluster. 63 are ultra-peripheral, tied only into it. The 59 connectors and 9 kinless notes are the ones that reach across. Only 11 notes are hubs, and there are no kinless hubs, so no single note dominates several clusters at once.

Betweenness asks a sharper question: which notes sit on the shortest paths between others. The top of that list is not what a list of important topics would look like.

The Library of Babel leads by a wide margin, 0.199 against 0.123 for the next note. It is a card tagged with philosophy, mathematics, existentialism and the human condition, and its body links to eight other notes, from Information Theory and Cryptography to Māyā and Free Will. Topics can be read the same way, by how evenly their notes spread across clusters. Philosophy and psychology are the widest bridges. At the other end, every one of the 31 machine-learning notes sits in a single cluster, as do all 16 introversion notes and all 13 tagged Noam Chomsky. Those topics are silos: written about in one sitting, from one source, and never linked out.

What predicts promotion

Promotion from inbox to cards is a human decision made one note at a time with no rule written down. The graph can show whether an implicit rule exists anyway.

The first finding is that the rate depends heavily on the cluster. Nine of ten UX notes are cards. None of the 28 notes in Mind & philosophy is, and 5 of 52 in mathematics and machine learning are. A chi-square test on the contingency table gives χ² = 80.9 on 12 degrees of freedom, p = 2.8e-12, Cramér's V = 0.48, a large effect for this kind of data.

Figure 13. Promotion rate by cluster.

To see how those combine into a rule, I fitted a depth-3 decision tree on structural features only, excluding word count, since length is close to the definition of an elaborated note. Classes were weighted to balance 97 cards against 317 inbox notes, every leaf had to hold at least 10 notes, and performance was measured with ten repeats of five-fold cross-validation and a 300-shuffle permutation test.

Figure 14. The learned promotion tree. Each leaf shows cards / inbox notes and the share promoted.

The tree reaches balanced accuracy 0.645 ± 0.063 and ROC AUC 0.71, against 0.52 for a stratified dummy. The permutation test gives p = 0.003. That is a real signal and a modest one.

The rule it finds is readable. The first split is whether a note bridges any clusters at all: of 192 notes with zero betweenness, 20 are cards; of 222 with any, 77 are. Among the bridges, a note with no backlinks and moderate tie strength is a card about as often as not (48 to 40). Among the non-bridges, a note with no topic at all is almost never promoted (1 in 85). The implicit rule is that I promote notes that connect things.

Adding word count and the My Thoughts flag raises balanced accuracy to 0.705 and AUC to 0.76, and word count takes 72% of the importance. My Thoughts takes none. Length predicts promotion better than position, which is expected, and position still carries information length does not.

None of these is a surprise to anyone who keeps a vault for six years. What the analysis adds is the size of each, and which ones actually change the structure. Label hygiene barely matters. Untagged imports and missing hand-written links matter a great deal.

Limitations

This is one person's vault, so nothing here generalises to how people think. It describes how one filing system represents one reader's reading. The concept layer is shaped by a template: a note with three topics is partly a note whose template asked for topics. Creation dates are mixed-source and include batch imports, which is why the growth analysis relies on order rather than timing. Word counts include text pasted from sources, so length measures elaboration only loosely. The promotion model is observational; it finds the rule my choices are consistent with, not the reason I made them. And community detection finds structure that is statistically real, but the names on the clusters are my reading of their topics.

Reproducing it

Seven scripts, run in order, against a copy of the vault. The parts that decide the result are short.

# 03_analysis.py: resource-allocation projection
for entity, notes in members.items():
    k = len(notes)
    if k < 2: continue
    w = 1.0 / (k - 1)                  # a shared hub means less
    for a, b in combinations(sorted(notes), 2):
